Portugal moves to double citizenship wait to 10 years for Nigerians and others

3 Nov 2026, 14:43 GMT

Portugal has approved regulations to apply its amended nationality law, which raises the minimum legal residence for citizenship from five to 10 years for nationals of countries including Nigeria. EU and Portuguese-speaking nationals face seven years.

Read more

Portugal is moving ahead with stricter citizenship rules that will double the waiting time for Nigerians and many other foreign residents, according to reports.

What changed

Portugal's Council of Ministers approved regulations on 29 October 2026 to put the country's amended Nationality Law into effect. Under the revised law, the minimum period of legal residence before applying for citizenship rises from five to 10 years for nationals of countries including Nigeria, India, Pakistan, Nepal and Bangladesh. Citizens of European Union countries and of Portuguese-speaking countries such as Angola, Cape Verde, Guinea-Bissau, São Tomé and Príncipe and Mozambique will have a seven-year route. The regulations also set out how language skills, cultural knowledge and financial independence will be assessed.

Not yet final

As of 2 November, the implementing decree had reportedly not yet been published in the official gazette, so some details of how the rules will apply remain unclear.

Background

Portugal has become a popular destination for migrants, including Nigerians, because of its relatively open residence routes. The government says the changes are meant to strengthen integration, while critics say they will make life harder for long-term residents. Hundreds of thousands of citizenship applications are reportedly waiting to be processed.

Why it matters

The change is the latest in a series of tighter immigration rules in Europe. This week the UK also confirmed a 10-year route to settlement for most lower-paid migrants.

US reportedly carries out limited strikes on Iran's Qeshm Island on eve of midterms

3 Nov 2026, 09:42 GMT

US officials say American forces carried out limited strikes on Iran's Qeshm Island near the Strait of Hormuz on Monday, in response to Iranian missile fire at US assets. Iranian state media reported explosions, while Iran claimed it captured two US drones.

Read more

The United States military has reportedly carried out limited strikes on Qeshm Island, a strategic Iranian island near the Strait of Hormuz, on Monday, 2 November 2026, the eve of the US midterm elections.

What happened

According to US officials quoted in reports, the operation was a defensive response after Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC) fired surface-to-air missiles at US assets. Iranian state media confirmed several explosions on the island. Officials reportedly said it was one of a series of low-profile strikes carried out in recent weeks, kept quiet to avoid signalling a return to full-scale fighting. Separately, the IRGC claimed it had intercepted and captured two US drones south of Qeshm.

Why Qeshm matters

Qeshm is the largest island in the Persian Gulf and is said to hold Iranian anti-ship missiles, sea mines and drones in underground tunnels. Its position near the Strait of Hormuz, through which a large share of the world's oil passes, makes it militarily important.

Diplomacy continues

The strikes came as Qatar leads indirect talks between the two sides. Iran's foreign minister, Abbas Araghchi, described the talks as active, and on Monday Iran said it could reopen the Strait of Hormuz within two days if the US met its conditions. US Vice President JD Vance reportedly said there had been progress, but warned that Iran remains unpredictable and that President Donald Trump would keep both military and diplomatic options open.

Why it matters

Oil prices rose slightly after reports of the strikes, with Brent crude trading at about $105 a barrel. Higher oil prices affect Nigeria's revenue and fuel costs around the world. The conflict between the US and Iran began on 28 February.

US to remove South Africa and Rwanda from AGOA duty-free trade scheme

3 Nov 2026, 09:42 GMT

The US says it will remove South Africa and Rwanda from the African Growth and Opportunity Act from 1 January 2027, accusing both of practices that break the scheme's rules. South Africa says it will support affected industries.

Read more

The United States plans to remove South Africa and Rwanda from the African Growth and Opportunity Act (AGOA), its main duty-free trade programme for African countries, from 1 January 2027.

What the US said

US Trade Representative Jamieson Greer said on Monday, 2 November 2026, that President Donald Trump intends to end the two countries' participation in the scheme. According to reports, Greer said the President found that both governments were engaging in practices inconsistent with AGOA's eligibility rules, including barriers to American trade and investment and actions that threaten US security and foreign policy interests in the region.

South Africa's response

South Africa's trade ministry said it would support the sectors affected and would keep pursuing trade and investment with the United States. It said AGOA remains important but is not the whole of the economic relationship between the two countries.

What is at stake

AGOA, enacted in 2000, gives eligible African countries duty-free access to the US market for thousands of products. South Africa has been its largest beneficiary, exporting about $1.7 billion worth of goods under the scheme in 2025, mainly car parts, citrus fruit and wine. Rwanda's main exports include coffee and minerals.

Background

The Trump administration has taken a series of steps against South Africa, accusing its government of persecuting white South Africans, a claim Pretoria rejects. Nigeria remains an AGOA beneficiary, although its exports under the scheme are dominated by oil. Trade experts say the decision is a warning to other African countries that eligibility can be withdrawn.

Police reportedly arrest suspect over shooting death of 15-year-old Ghanaian boy Javits Frimpong in New York

2 Nov 2026, 23:03 GMT

Police have reportedly made an arrest in connection with the shooting of Javits Frimpong, a 15-year-old Ghanaian boy who was killed while walking to school in the Bronx, New York. Questions remain about what led to the shooting and whether others were involved.

Police have reportedly made an arrest in connection with the tragic shooting of Javits Frimpong, a 15-year-old Ghanaian teenager who was killed in the Bronx, New York.

The development reportedly came after officers spent much of the afternoon searching for suspected gang members believed to have been involved in the incident.

What happened

Javits was shot and killed while walking to school in the Bronx last week. He had moved from Ghana about a year earlier to live with his father, Thomas Frimpong, in New York.

His father has said he believes bullying may have been behind the attack, and police have been looking into it as a possible motive.

Grief and calls for justice

The killing has caused deep grief in the Ghanaian community and beyond. Students in the Bronx staged a walkout after his death, a memorial in his honour has continued to grow, and the Ghanaian government has vowed to pursue justice for the boy.

Questions remain

Although one suspect is now reportedly in custody, several questions remain unanswered, including what exactly led to the shooting and whether other people were involved. The identity of the person reportedly arrested has not been officially confirmed.

Police investigations are expected to continue as officers try to piece together what happened and establish who was responsible.

A life cut short

For Javits Frimpong's family, no arrest can bring back the young life that has been lost. At just 15 years old, he had so much of his future ahead of him.

The hope now is that a thorough investigation will uncover the truth and ensure that anyone found responsible is brought to justice.
