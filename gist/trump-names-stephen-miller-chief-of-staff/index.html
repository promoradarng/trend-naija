Trump names Stephen Miller White House chief of staff after midterm losses

7 Nov 2026, 06:43 GMT

US President Donald Trump has appointed Stephen Miller as White House chief of staff, replacing Susie Wiles following the Republican Party's heavy losses in this week's midterm elections.

What happened

A White House official said Wiles resigned on Thursday night, and Trump announced the change on his Truth Social platform, describing Miller as one of his most trusted advisers. He said Wiles would move to his political operation and thanked her for her service. Asked by reporters on Friday, 6 November 2026, why he had made the change, Trump said he wanted to make a change. Before the elections, he had repeatedly said there was no chance he would replace Wiles.

Who is Stephen Miller

Miller, 41, has been one of the most influential figures in Trump's administrations, serving as deputy chief of staff for policy and homeland security adviser. He played a central role in designing the mass deportation programme that became a defining policy of Trump's second term, which has drawn protests and legal challenges, including over the conduct of immigration agents.

Democrats react

Senate Democratic leader Chuck Schumer described Miller as the architect of the administration's harshest policies, said Trump could not have made a worse choice, and promised that the new Democratic majorities in Congress would fight him every step of the way.

Background

In the midterm elections on 3 November, the Democrats were projected to win control of both the House of Representatives and the Senate, a result that is expected to make it much harder for Trump to pass legislation in the last two years of his term. Wiles, who ran Trump's 2024 campaign, was the first woman to serve as White House chief of staff.

  • Miller designed Trump's mass deportation programme.
  • Democrats now control both houses of Congress.

Guinea deports 1,557 West Africans convicted of illegal gold mining

16 Nov 2026, 06:42 GMT

Guinea has deported 1,557 West Africans, mostly from Burkina Faso and Mali, who had been jailed for illegal gold mining, as it steps up a crackdown on unauthorised mining.

Read more

Guinea has expelled 1,557 West African nationals who were arrested and convicted for illegal gold mining.

The deportation

According to judicial and security sources quoted in reports on Sunday, 15 November 2026, the deportees, mostly citizens of Burkina Faso and Mali, were arrested at mining sites during security operations. After serving prison sentences of 18 months to two years, they were taken in a convoy of 44 vehicles from the capital, Conakry, to the border town of Kouremale on the Mali border, arriving on Saturday.

The crackdown

Guinean authorities stepped up operations against illegal mining in early November, particularly in Siguiri, a major gold-producing region in the north-east. The government says the aim is to stop illegal exploitation of mineral resources and limit environmental damage from unregulated mining.

Background

The crackdown has also raised tensions between miners and the authorities.

The verdict

The State Security Court on Sunday, 15 November 2026, found the men, two brothers and a relative by marriage, guilty of terrorist attacks resulting in death, weapons offences, conspiracy to commit terrorist acts and promoting the ideas of a terrorist organisation, according to a statement from the Jordanian military.

The attack

Gunmen killed nine people and wounded 25 when they stormed a funeral at a Greek Orthodox church in Hammoud village, in the Karak governorate, on 23 October 2026. The three suspects fled after the attack and were arrested three days later.

When Congo turned on Lumumba, Egypt did not abandon his family: how Nasser sent a colonel to save his children

14 Nov 2026, 14:31 GMT

As Patrice Lumumba's government collapsed and his life came under threat, Egypt's President Gamal Abdel Nasser ordered a secret mission led by Colonel Saad El-Din El-Shazly to get Lumumba's children out of Congo. They grew up in Cairo under Egypt's protection.

Read more

It is one of the lesser-known chapters of Africa's liberation story, and a remarkable act of solidarity between two African nations.

A leader in danger

Patrice Lumumba, Congo's first Prime Minister after independence from Belgium in 1960, was overthrown within months and placed under house arrest. In January 1961 he was handed over to his enemies and assassinated, one of the most shocking political killings in African history.

As Congo descended into chaos, Lumumba's family was in grave danger, and his wife Pauline and their children had nowhere safe to turn.

Nasser's secret mission

Egypt did not look away. On the direct orders of President Gamal Abdel Nasser, Colonel Saad El-Din El-Shazly, who later became one of Egypt's most celebrated military commanders, took on the task of getting Lumumba's children safely out of the country.

El-Shazly knew Congo well. He had commanded the Egyptian battalion sent there in 1960 as part of the international force during the independence crisis, at a time when Egypt firmly backed Lumumba.

The operation was carried out in complete secrecy. Lumumba's children, including his eldest son François, were smuggled out of Congo and flown to Cairo, where they arrived safely while their father was still under arrest.

A new home in Cairo

After Lumumba's death, Pauline joined the children in Egypt. Nasser offered the family protection and support, and the Egyptian government helped with their housing, schooling and other needs.

Lumumba's children grew up in Egypt, which became not just a place of refuge but a home that embraced their father's cause as a symbol of African liberation.

Pauline later spent time in Europe before returning to Congo in the late 1960s, after Lumumba began to be honoured as a national hero. She settled in Kinshasa.

Africa looking out for its own

The story is a reminder that Egypt was not just a witness to Africa's independence struggles, but an active partner in them. When Lumumba's family was in danger, an African nation stepped in to protect them.
