Police reportedly arrest suspect over shooting death of 15-year-old Ghanaian boy Javits Frimpong in New York

2 Nov 2026, 23:03 GMT

Police have reportedly made an arrest in connection with the shooting of Javits Frimpong, a 15-year-old Ghanaian boy who was killed while walking to school in the Bronx, New York. Questions remain about what led to the shooting and whether others were involved.

Read more

Police have reportedly made an arrest in connection with the tragic shooting of Javits Frimpong, a 15-year-old Ghanaian teenager who was killed in the Bronx, New York.

The development reportedly came after officers spent much of the afternoon searching for suspected gang members believed to have been involved in the incident.

What happened

Javits was shot and killed while walking to school in the Bronx last week. He had moved from Ghana about a year earlier to live with his father, Thomas Frimpong, in New York.

His father has said he believes bullying may have been behind the attack, and police have been looking into it as a possible motive.

Grief and calls for justice

The killing has caused deep grief in the Ghanaian community and beyond. Students in the Bronx staged a walkout after his death, a memorial in his honour has continued to grow, and the Ghanaian government has vowed to pursue justice for the boy.

Questions remain

Although one suspect is now reportedly in custody, several questions remain unanswered, including what exactly led to the shooting and whether other people were involved. The identity of the person reportedly arrested has not been officially confirmed.

Police investigations are expected to continue as officers try to piece together what happened and establish who was responsible.

A life cut short

For Javits Frimpong's family, no arrest can bring back the young life that has been lost. At just 15 years old, he had so much of his future ahead of him.

The hope now is that a thorough investigation will uncover the truth and ensure that anyone found responsible is brought to justice.

Iran says it could reopen Strait of Hormuz within two days if US meets its demands

2 Nov 2026, 20:41 GMT

Iran has reportedly said it could reopen the Strait of Hormuz within 48 hours if Washington accepts its conditions, including lifting oil sanctions and ending a naval blockade. Talks in Doha ended without a breakthrough.

Read more

Iran says it could reopen the Strait of Hormuz, one of the world's most important oil shipping routes, within 48 hours if the United States accepts its conditions for ending the war between the two countries.

What Iran said

Foreign Ministry spokesman Esmaeil Baghaei said on Monday, 2 November 2026, that Tehran had shortened an earlier seven-day proposal to two days, and reportedly described this as a sign of good faith. He said reopening the waterway and returning to negotiations depended on Washington agreeing to Iran's demands, and that messages were still being exchanged through mediators.

Iran's conditions

According to reports, Iran wants an end to the war across the Middle East, the release of its frozen assets abroad, the lifting of sanctions on its oil sector and an end to the US naval blockade of its ports. Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi has also called for a new transit arrangement for ships passing through the strait, agreed between Iran and Oman, and fewer US troops in the region.

Background

The two countries have been in conflict for about eight months. Iranian and Qatari negotiators held three days of talks in Doha aimed at reaching a deal, but they ended without a breakthrough. On Sunday, President Donald Trump told a rally that the US might eventually have to give Iran 'the hammer', and Iran's army said it was ready for a long war.

Why it matters

A large share of the world's oil passes through the Strait of Hormuz. Disruption there affects global oil prices, which matters for Nigeria's earnings from crude oil and for fuel costs around the world.

UK drops plan to make care workers wait 15 years for settlement

2 Nov 2026, 18:01 GMT

UK Home Secretary Shabana Mahmood has reportedly dropped a proposal that would have made some migrant care workers wait 15 years for indefinite leave to remain. Care workers who joined before May 2025 and earn at least £25,000 keep a five-year route, while most other lower earners face 10 years.

Read more

The UK government has reportedly dropped a plan that would have required some migrant care workers already in Britain to wait 15 years before they could settle permanently, a change that affects many Nigerians working in the sector.

What changed

Home Secretary Shabana Mahmood told the House of Commons on Monday, 2 November 2026, that care workers who joined the social care visa route before May 2025 and earn at least £25,000 a year in the sector will keep a five-year route to indefinite leave to remain. She also scrapped the proposed 15-year wait for other lower-paid workers, replacing it with a 10-year route.

The new rules

Mahmood said settlement will now be more closely linked to a person's contribution, adding that the right to live in Britain must be earned. A five-year route will remain for people earning more than £50,000 a year and for qualifying workers in health and education. People earning more than £125,000 could settle in three years. Most other migrants, including those earning under £50,000, would face a 10-year route, while being able to renew their temporary visas.

Why the plan was changed

The reversal followed opposition from Labour MPs, trade unions and migrant groups, who argued it was unfair to change the rules for people who came to Britain under different conditions.

What it means for Nigerians

Nigerians are among the largest groups of overseas care workers recruited to the UK in recent years. Indefinite leave to remain allows a person to live, work and study in Britain without time limits and claim benefits where eligible, and is usually a step towards citizenship.

Hackers send fake nuclear attack alerts to millions in Russia and Ukraine

2 Nov 2026, 14:56 GMT

Emergency warning services in Russia and Ukraine were reportedly hacked to send false alerts of nuclear, chemical and radiation threats. Authorities in both countries confirmed the incidents and said their systems had been restored.

Read more

Emergency alert services in both Russia and Ukraine were reportedly hit by cyber attacks that sent false warnings of nuclear, chemical and radiation threats to civilians, according to reports on Monday, 2 November 2026.

In Russia

Fake nuclear attack warnings appeared on channels linked to Russia's Emergency Situations Ministry in Moscow, St Petersburg and several other regions at about 2.45am, reportedly telling residents to gather three weeks' supply of food and take shelter in cellars. Similar messages were reported on the ministry's mobile app, by text message and on the state-backed messaging platform MAX. The ministry described it as a massive cyber attack, but said its sirens, loudspeakers, television and radio warning systems had not been affected. It has not been established who carried out the attack.

In Ukraine

Hours earlier, Ukraine's Air Alert service sent a series of false notifications claiming radiation and chemical threats in Kyiv, a nuclear alert across the country and street fighting in the capital, and telling residents to stay indoors. Ukrainian authorities confirmed the service had been hacked and said it was working normally again after midnight. The app normally warns people of incoming Russian missile and drone attacks, often giving them only minutes to find shelter. Hours after the false alerts, real warnings sounded during a Russian attack on Odesa that reportedly injured four people.

Background

The incidents came amid continued strikes by both sides and talk of possible negotiations. A Kremlin adviser said he believed President Vladimir Putin would take a phone call from President Volodymyr Zelensky, although Putin has refused his calls in the past.

Why it matters

Experts warn that attacks on alert systems are dangerous because they can cause panic and make people less likely to trust genuine warnings.
