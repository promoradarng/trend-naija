US congressman introduces bill to stop lobbyists working for Nigeria

31 Oct 2026, 14:43 GMT

US Representative Barry Moore has introduced a bill that would bar American lobbyists from being paid to represent Nigeria while it remains designated a Country of Particular Concern over religious freedom. He accuses the Nigerian government of paying lobbyists to counter criticism over attacks on Christians.

Read more

A United States congressman has introduced legislation aimed at stopping American lobbying firms from working for the Nigerian government.

What the bill does

Representative Barry Moore introduced H.R. 10939, which would bar people and firms in the United States from accepting payment to lobby on Nigeria's behalf for as long as Nigeria is designated a Country of Particular Concern (CPC) for religious freedom violations. Firms that take such payments during that period could face sanctions. The bill was reported on Saturday, 31 October 2026, and has been referred to the House Committee on Foreign Affairs.

Why Moore introduced it

Moore reportedly accused the Nigerian government of paying expensive lobbyists in Washington to play down the persecution of Christians in the country. The allegations of persecution are disputed by the Nigerian government, which has said that insecurity affects people of all faiths and is driven by terrorism, banditry and communal conflicts.

Background

The United States designated Nigeria a Country of Particular Concern, a label for countries accused of severe violations of religious freedom. The designation has strained relations between the two countries, and the Nigerian government has hired lobbying firms in Washington to present its position. The issue gained new attention this week after a Christian woman was convicted in Borno State over converting an adult, a case criticised by Christian groups.

What happens next

The bill must pass through committee and both houses of Congress before it can become law. The Nigerian government had not responded publicly at the time of the report.

Pope Leo XIV casts mail-in ballot in US midterm elections

30 Oct 2026, 20:49 GMT

The Vatican says Pope Leo XIV has voted by mail in the US midterm elections in his home state of Illinois, believed to be the first time a pope has voted in an American election. The Vatican said he believes citizens have a right and duty to take part in democracy.

Read more

Pope Leo XIV, the first American pope, has voted in the United States midterm elections, the Vatican has confirmed.

What happened

The Holy See press office said on Friday, 30 October 2026, that the Pope had cast a mail-in ballot in Illinois, where he was born. It said he had exercised his right to vote as a US citizen whenever he was eligible, and that he believes citizens have both a right and a duty to take part in democratic processes. His ballot is expected to include races for the US Senate, the governorship of Illinois and the House of Representatives. He is believed to be the first pope ever to vote in an American election.

Background

The Pope was born Robert Francis Prevost in Chicago and holds both American and Peruvian citizenship, having spent many years as a missionary and bishop in Peru. He was elected pope in May 2025 after the death of Pope Francis. Public records show he voted regularly in Illinois before becoming pope, including in the 2024 presidential election. The Vatican did not say who he voted for.

Why it matters

The US midterm elections in November will decide control of Congress, and the Pope's decision to vote has drawn wide attention because of his role as a global religious leader. Supporters praised him for setting an example of civic duty, while others questioned whether a pope should take part in a national election.

What happens next

The Pope is expected to continue his public schedule, including a planned visit to South America.

Kemi Badenoch hits back after being called a 'Nigerian scammer' online

30 Oct 2026, 11:49 GMT

UK Conservative leader Kemi Badenoch has condemned online attacks referring to her Nigerian background, after a former adviser linked to Nigel Farage repeatedly called her a scammer. She said Britain is not a racist country and the attacks would not work, while Farage said the comment was over the top but not racist.

Read more

Kemi Badenoch, the Nigerian-raised leader of Britain's Conservative Party, has condemned online attacks that referred to her heritage, saying they show what kind of people her critics are.

What happened

According to reports on Friday, 30 October 2026, Raheem Kassam, a former adviser linked to Nigel Farage's camp, repeatedly called Badenoch a scammer in social media posts over the weekend, mocking her Nigerian background and warning people not to give her their bank details. He also accused her of using her heritage to advance her career when it suited her.

What Badenoch said

Speaking on a British breakfast television programme, Badenoch said some people in Reform UK appeared to think that attacking her race would help them. She said Britain is not a racist country and that such attacks would not work, and urged former Conservative voters who had switched to Reform to come back.

Reform's response

Robert Jenrick, a former Conservative MP who has defected to Reform UK, reportedly declined to call the comments racist, describing them as perhaps rude or unpleasant. Badenoch said Jenrick was now Nigel Farage's problem. Farage said that mentioning that someone grew up in Nigeria was not racist, but later described Kassam's comment as over the top. A Labour spokesperson criticised Jenrick for not condemning the remarks.

Background

Badenoch was born in London, grew up partly in Lagos and moved back to Britain as a teenager. She became Conservative leader in 2024. Reform UK has been rising in opinion polls, and several former Conservatives have joined the party, increasing tensions between the two.

US court rejects bid to seek evidence from Tinubu in FOIA case, Presidency says

31 Oct 2026, 17:44 GMT

A federal court in Washington has reportedly refused a request by US researcher Aaron Greenspan to seek evidence from President Bola Tinubu and a lobbying firm in his records lawsuit against the FBI and DEA. The Presidency welcomed the ruling, while the underlying case continues.

Read more

A United States federal court has turned down an attempt to obtain evidence from President Bola Tinubu as part of a long-running records lawsuit, according to a statement from the Presidency on Saturday, 31 October 2026.

What the court decided

The case, before the US District Court for the District of Columbia, is a Freedom of Information Act lawsuit filed by American researcher Aaron Greenspan against the Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI) and the Drug Enforcement Administration (DEA). On 30 October, the court reportedly denied Greenspan's request for limited discovery from President Tinubu and the lobbying firm DCI Group, which he had linked to alleged cyberattacks on his website. The court found the request was not relevant enough to the main records dispute. It also rejected a request by a third party to file a friend-of-the-court brief, refused a related request for judicial notice, and denied Greenspan's motion for summary judgment, meaning the main case continues.

The Presidency's reaction

The President's adviser on information and strategy, Bayo Onanuga, announced the development. Lawyers quoted in the statement said the ruling kept the case focused on the issues properly before the court and set clear limits on what it would consider.

Background

Greenspan has for several years sought the release of US government records he says relate to investigations involving the President in the 1990s. The President's lawyers have consistently opposed the release of the records, and the Presidency has dismissed the claims linked to them. The case has drawn wide attention in Nigeria, particularly from opposition supporters.

What happens next

The FOIA case itself will continue, and further rulings are expected. Greenspan had not publicly responded to the decision at the time of the report.
