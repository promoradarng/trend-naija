The new price

The new rate is ₦1,159 per litre, down from ₦1,210, a cut of about 4.2 per cent. The reduction gives marketers and filling station owners room to lower pump prices, although retail prices also depend on transport, distribution costs and each retailer's margins.

Marketers have warned, however, that renewed tension between Iran and the United States could push global prices up again.

Read more

A governorship campaign rally organised by the All Progressives Congress (APC) in Offa, Kwara State, has reportedly descended into chaos following claims that some participants were not paid the mobilisation allowances they had been promised.

What reportedly happened

According to reports, the rally drew a large crowd of supporters to the streets of Offa. But tension rose when some of those who had turned up complained that they had not received the money they were allegedly promised for attending.

The situation reportedly turned violent as angry participants confronted organisers, disrupting the event.

The full details, including whether anyone was injured and how much money was involved, have not been independently confirmed, and the party has not publicly commented on the incident.

The business of crowds

The incident shines a light on a practice many Nigerians already know about: paying people, often called "mobilisation", to attend political rallies.

Across parties, campaign organisers frequently provide transport, food, branded caps and T-shirts, and sometimes cash, to fill venues and create the impression of massive support. When those promises are not kept, frustration can quickly boil over.

It also raises a deeper question. If people have to be paid to attend, how much do the crowds at political rallies really tell us about genuine support?

The figures

The UK's trade and investment factsheet, released on 6 November 2026 and reported this weekend, shows Britain bought £1.02 billion worth of refined petroleum products from Nigeria in the 12 months to June 2026. Why it matters

Read more

It is one of the lesser-known chapters of Africa's liberation story, and a remarkable act of solidarity between two African nations.

A leader in danger

Patrice Lumumba, Congo's first Prime Minister after independence from Belgium in 1960, was overthrown within months and placed under house arrest. In January 1961 he was handed over to his enemies and assassinated, one of the most shocking political killings in African history.

As Congo descended into chaos, Lumumba's family was in grave danger, and his wife Pauline and their children had nowhere safe to turn.

Nasser's secret mission

Egypt did not look away. On the direct orders of President Gamal Abdel Nasser, Colonel Saad El-Din El-Shazly, who later became one of Egypt's most celebrated military commanders, took on the task of getting Lumumba's children safely out of the country.

El-Shazly knew Congo well. He had commanded the Egyptian battalion sent there in 1960 as part of the international force during the independence crisis, at a time when Egypt firmly backed Lumumba.

The operation was carried out in complete secrecy. Lumumba's children, including his eldest son François, were smuggled out of Congo and flown to Cairo, where they arrived safely while their father was still under arrest.

A new home in Cairo

After Lumumba's death, Pauline joined the children in Egypt. Nasser offered the family protection and support, and the Egyptian government helped with their housing, schooling and other needs.

Lumumba's children grew up in Egypt, which became not just a place of refuge but a home that embraced their father's cause as a symbol of African liberation.

Pauline later spent time in Europe before returning to Congo in the late 1960s, after Lumumba began to be honoured as a national hero. She settled in Kinshasa.

Africa looking out for its own

The story is a reminder that Egypt was not just a witness to Africa's independence struggles, but an active partner in them. When Lumumba's family was in danger, an African nation stepped in to protect them.
