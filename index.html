Nigeria's Flamingos have reached the final of the FIFA U-17 Women's World Cup for the first time after beating Brazil 3-1 in the semi-final. Awawu Bashiru scored twice and Queen Joseph once. They face Japan in the final.

Read more

Nigeria's under-17 women's team, the Flamingos, have made history by reaching the final of the FIFA U-17 Women's World Cup for the first time.

The match

The Flamingos beat Brazil 3-1 in the semi-final on Wednesday night, 4 November 2026, according to reports. Queen Joseph opened the scoring in the 56th minute, and Awawu Bashiru doubled the lead in the 71st minute before adding her second goal in the 80th minute to put the game beyond Brazil's reach.

A record run

The win beats the team's previous best performance, a bronze medal at the 2022 edition of the tournament. On the way to the semi-final, the Flamingos beat Venezuela in the quarter-finals, a result that earned praise from the Confederation of African Football.

The final

The Flamingos will now face Japan in the final as they chase Nigeria's first FIFA U-17 Women's World Cup title. Japan, one of the strongest sides in women's youth football, won the tournament in 2014.

Background

The result comes at a difficult time for Nigerian women's football at senior level. The Super Falcons failed to qualify for the Women's World Cup for the first time, and no Nigerian player was shortlisted for this year's CAF Women's Player of the Year award. The Flamingos' success has been welcomed as a sign that a new generation of players is coming through.

FIFA's message

FIFA congratulated the Flamingos on qualifying for the final, a first for Nigeria at this level of the women's game.

How people reacted

Nigerians celebrated the win across social media, praising the young players and their coaches and urging the government and the football federation to support them ahead of the final.

The decisions

The decisions were taken at the council's 112th meeting on 3 November, chaired by the Chief Justice of Nigeria, Justice Kudirat Kekere-Ekun. The council also issued cautions to eight others and approved warning letters to three judges following investigations into petitions about their conduct. The offences included unlawful detention of litigants, abuse of judicial authority and breaches of due process.

Who was suspended

Those suspended were reportedly Justice Hannatu Mohammed Kabir of the Nasarawa State High Court, Grand Kadi Mohammed Sani Salihu of the Jigawa State Sharia Court of Appeal and Justice Sobere Biambo of the Rivers State High Court. Background

The NJC is the body responsible for disciplining judges in Nigeria. Public trust in the judiciary has been a recurring concern, particularly over election cases and conflicting court orders. Observers say the decisions send a signal that judges will be held accountable for abusing their powers.

Reps bill would let regulators shut hospitals that refuse emergency patients

5 Nov 2026, 06:43 GMT

A bill to amend the National Health Act has passed second reading in the House of Representatives. It would require hospitals to begin triage within 15 minutes and stabilise emergency patients within 30 minutes, or risk losing their licences.

Read more

A bill that would allow regulators to suspend or revoke the licences of hospitals that refuse or delay emergency treatment has passed its second reading in the House of Representatives.

What the bill says

The bill, sponsored by House spokesman Akin Rotimi and debated on Wednesday, 4 November 2026, seeks to amend the National Health Act 2014 by setting mandatory timelines for emergency care. Under the proposal, facilities would have to begin assessing a patient within 15 minutes of arrival and provide basic stabilisation within 30 minutes. Where a life-threatening condition is identified, staff would have to start lifesaving treatment immediately, and hospitals with a free bed would have to assign it to an emergency patient.

Why it is needed

Section 20 of the existing Act already bans health workers and facilities from refusing emergency treatment, with a fine of ₦100,000, up to six months in prison, or both. However, some hospitals have continued to turn emergency patients away or demand payment before treatment. Rotimi said delays, administrative hurdles, upfront payment demands and poor referrals were still causing avoidable complications and deaths, and that the amendment would make clear who is responsible and how quickly they must act.

Background

There have been many reports in Nigeria of accident and gunshot victims, pregnant women and others dying after being refused care because they could not pay a deposit or provide a police report. The government has previously said hospitals must treat gunshot victims without waiting for police clearance.

Senate version

A similar bill sponsored by Senator Abdulaziz Yar'Adua is before the Senate. It proposes up to six months in prison, a fine of 300,000 naira, or both, for hospitals and health workers that fail to provide emergency care. It would also require hospitals to assess patients within 15 minutes, begin stabilisation within 30 minutes, provide a bed within two hours where available, and dispatch an ambulance or other transport within 30 minutes when a referral is needed. The medical and nursing councils would be asked to set up a national emergency care curriculum.

What happens next

The bill will now go to committee for further work before returning to the House for third reading.

The Director-General of the Department of State Services (DSS), Oluwatosin Adeola Ajayi, has ordered the release of four men who had been accused of belonging to the proscribed Indigenous People of Biafra (IPOB).

Who they are

The four men, Emele Uduma Ibe, Berefgagha Appah, Ondokeyilemi Aggry and Oyeinmokebebeton Aggry, were arrested in May 2022 in the Sampou and Akeddei communities of Kolokuma/Opokuma and Sagbama Local Government Areas of Bayelsa State. That means they spent about four and a half years in custody before their release.

₦5 million each

After they were cleared of the allegations, the DG approved an initial compensation package of ₦20 million, with each man receiving ₦5 million. The men are also expected to receive psychological and medical support and help returning to normal life.

Security sources said investigations showed the men had been lured by people claiming to be IPOB members, who promised them ₦100,000 a month under the Federal Government's Amnesty Programme.

"Where we admit our mistakes, we offer compensation, and seek ways to improve operations in tandem with the rule of law," Ajayi said. One of the freed men, Emele Uduma Ibe, described the day as the happiest of his life.

Not the first time

This is not the first such case under Ajayi. After he took office in 2024, he ordered a review of more than 300 inherited terrorism cases. Others who have since been compensated include a Jos businessman shot in error by DSS personnel, who received ₦20 million, and several people cleared of IPOB ties who received between ₦5 million and ₦10 million.

Can money repair the damage?

For anyone who has spent years in detention over an allegation they did not commit, freedom alone may not be enough to repair the damage. There is the emotional suffering, the disruption of family life, the loss of income and the stigma that often follows such accusations.

The ₦5 million payment may provide some financial relief, but it cannot replace the time lost.

Still, the decision sends an important message about accountability within Nigeria's security system. Security agencies have a duty to investigate threats to national security and protect citizens, but that duty must be carried out within the law and with respect for the rights of people who have not been convicted of any offence.

Many Nigerians will be watching to see whether similar attention is given to other people who may have been wrongly accused or detained, especially given the controversies around IPOB-linked arrests in the South-East and South-South.
